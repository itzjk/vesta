To the Moon and Back Again

Houston We Have a Podcast
Season 1Episode 440Oct 9, 2026

From Earth orbit to the Moon and Mars, explore the world of human spaceflight with NASA each week on the official podcast of the Johnson Space Center in Houston, Texas. Listen to in-depth conversations with the astronauts, scientists and engineers who make it possible.

On episode 440, NASA’s Artemis II crew reflect on their historic journey around the Moon, the moments that moved them, and what it’s like to come home after traveling farther than any humans before them. This episode was recorded in July and August 2026.

Transcript

Nilufar Ramji

Houston We Have a Podcast. Welcome to the official podcast of the NASA Johnson Space Center, episode 440: To the Moon and Back Again. I’m Nilufar Ramji, and I’ll be your host today. On this podcast, we bring in the experts, scientists, engineers, and astronauts, all to let you know what’s going on in the world of human spaceflight and more.

On April 1, 2026, Artemis II launched on a nearly 10-day voyage around the Moon, marking the first crewed flight of NASA’s Orion spacecraft. NASA astronauts Reid Wiseman, Victor Glover, and Christina Koch, along with Canadian Space Agency astronaut Jeremy Hansen, splashed down on April 10th in the Pacific Ocean off the coast of San Diego. At their farthest point, the crew traveled 252,756 miles from Earth, setting a record for the greatest distance humans have traveled in space and observing the lunar surface like never before.

Since they’ve been back, the crew have been on a whirlwind tour, sharing their experiences with the world. And of course, we had to have them back on Houston We Have a Podcast. We heard from the crew when they were selected for Artemis II on episode 283, and then right before they flew on episode 417. Through these interviews we’ve had a chance to get to know them right here on the podcast. Coming back from their journey, we sat down with each of the crew individually to hear their reflections, and we laughed, we cried, and everything in between.

I hope you enjoy these conversations after their historical mission.

Let’s go for our crew, our teammates, and all humanity.

[Music]

Reid, welcome back. It’s been a little over three months since the Artemis II mission, and that mission took you around the Moon. So first off, tell us what that was like.

 

Reid Wiseman 

It was the greatest adventure that four humans can ever go on.

I, I mean, we know the crew well. I was with Victor, Christina, and Jeremy this morning. We’re still bonded, just like we were during the mission, and getting to see some of the sights- I guess the biggest, the biggest surprise to me, being a space station flyer on this, I knew the crew was going to be good. I knew mission control was going to be good. I was pretty sure the vehicle was going to be good. The thing that blew my mind was how quickly we left Earth. I mean, it was just gone, especially on the second, third day. It just was so small and getting smaller. It was this little crescent out the window, and I was, from my time in the space station, it was just shocking to me. I didn’t expect that. I didn’t expect us to just be gone so quickly.

 

Nilufar Ramji

Just disappears in the background, and you know, everyone will ask you about launch, but was there a quiet moment, maybe days later, the next day, when you suddenly thought, “I’m actually on my way to the Moon”?

 

Reid Wiseman

I think we’ve started to talk about this a little bit as a crew. And for me, I don’t know if it was a protectionary element in my brain, but I kind of thought at all times before every one of these major burns that, that if something didn’t go right, we were going to go home.

So we launch off of the Cape, off of Pad 39B at the Cape, and we go out to our first apogee where we need to raise our perigee or we’re going to come straight home. And in my mind, it was like, “well, if this doesn’t go great, we’re going to be home in 45 minutes…” And then it went great. And then we did our our giant apogee raise burn, and we’re going out to 38,000 miles, 24 hour orbit. And I thought at that at the peak of that orbit, I was like, “All right, well, if it’s not going great, we’re just going to go home.” And then we did our perigee raise burn, and it went great, and we weren’t going home.

And then our next step is trans lunar injection, and heading into that burn, Jeremy and Christina were at controls the vehicle. Victor and I were supporting, and we had a few we had a few things go a little bit wrong as we’re cruising through the Van Allen belts. Christina lost her computer, and so she had to go to backup procedures. And then we had a a cabin leak suspected warning come up, and that got us our adrenaline going. So we’re like 20 minutes from trans lunar injection, and we have a cabin leak suspected. And I’m just kind of sitting there, like maybe we are actually going to go home on this one. But everything was looking good, and Mission Control did a superb job of cleaning that up. And then we did trans lunar injection, and then it was kind of then that all of those thoughts of “we’re just going to go home,” they went into the background, and now we’re not going home, “we’re going to the Moon,” and from that point on, I never thought about anything else other than “we’re going to the Moon.”

 

Nilufar Ramji 

See you and the crew. I keep going back to Christina’s words: planet Earth. You are our crew. That’s one of my favorite lines post flight, among many others. But your. Crew aboard Orion really stood out to the world for its camaraderie. Is trust like that something you can deliberately build as a crew, or is it something that you discover when things get hard?

 

Reid Wiseman 

Wow, that is a that is a great question. As you were using the word trust, I was thinking in the back of my head, the word earned. I think you can set out with intentionality to build trust. That’s a great thing. It’s a noble cause. But I think at the end of the day, trust has to be earned.

And I’m going to take a little bit of risk here with this this conversation, but this crew put in a tremendous amount of work leading up to this flight. And for those who are close to us, they know we weren’t always friends, and we we weren’t always aligned. We we definitely were not always in agreement. That made us stronger in the end, actually. But I never really knew how this crew was going to show up when when the rocket lit, when we were weightless, when we were on our way to the Moon, when we were doing real life or death high risk elements. And honestly, the first window I got into that was I had opened up an oatmeal and I hadn’t hydrated it enough, and so there was like there was so much oatmeal floating out of this bag, like 10,000 pieces of oatmeal is an underestimate. It’s a cloud of oatmeal floating away from me, and I was just looking at it, and I’m like, “I’ve I’ve spent six months at the International Space Station. I should not I should not be this bad at astronauting.”

 

Nilufar Ramji 

“Astronauting.” New verb!

 

Reid Wiseman 

And I’m looking at this cloud, and and my first instinct was I got to clean this up, and then it was just so much. I was like, all right, just let it float because there’s just no solving this. And and Victor turns around, he’s just like, “no problem,” and he just starts kind of gathering it up and eating some of it and pushing some of it over towards the HEPA filter and letting it, you know, get stuck to the filtration system, which is totally safe and completely reasonable. But I think for me personally, that was a moment for me when I knew we were all we had that trust and that and that went across the whole crew and that sort of grace towards each other on this mission in the end resulted in the ultimate trust between the four of us.

 

Nilufar Ramji 

What a great answer! You may have done this a time or two.

 

Reid Wiseman 

I did. You could tell. I turned my paper upside down. I don’t even want to know the questions. I just want to have a conversation.

 

Nilufar Ramji 

I love that, and I’m using the questions as a guiding parameter. But more than anything, I want to talk a little bit now about your role as commander. So, as leaders in any kind of organization, we’re always looking to lead in certain areas and then have our team complete tasks in other areas, right? So, during a mission, when there are 1000s of decisions to be made, different procedures, you’re time lined up for the day, how do you know which of those decisions deserves your attention, and which ones do you intentionally leave to your team?

 

Reid Wiseman

What an unbelievably difficult question to answer. Maybe I shouldn’t have flipped my piece of paper over. It’s it’s a great question, and I first I think I would just love to talk about followership and teamwork, and and you said as the commander of the the mission that almost made me a little bit embarrassed because I really was just a member of this unbelievable crew. And at any time during this mission, every ship needs a captain, I get that. But I think if if we were sitting here with Victor, Christina, and Jeremy, I would hope they would say like we all were leading in that mission, and we all had a lot of responsibility at a lot of different times. And if you actually dissected the work in the mission, my hope would be that I was almost the fourth crew member, and Christina and Jeremy were running almost everything on orbit. Victor was running just about everything on ascent and entry. We worked really deliberately to build out this team and give everybody real meaningful work and meaningful responsibility.

But back to your question of like, how do you know? You don’t know. I’ve spent a lot of my time in the cockpit of aircraft. I went through test pilot school. I think Navy test pilot school is probably the time when I started to most understand if you got 1000 things thrown at you at once, try to find the one thing that is life or death. Focus on that first, and then maybe the next like… 10 things that you got to get done soon, and then everything else can become… I wish Matt Dominick was here. It could become a “future Reid Wiseman problem.” He always says that. Like this is a “future Matt Dominick” problem. Like I’ll worry about that one tomorrow.

But we developed a little saying in the crew. Victor brought it up the first time when we were trying to figure out. We’re the first humans to fly this. And our first action can be right, and it can save our lives, or it can be really wrong, and that could really hurt us. So we we develop like no fast hands in the cockpit, like go slow, let’s be cerebral and methodical about our decisions. But at the end of the day, if there is an emergency that needs an immediate action, something that must happen right now, we develop the mantra of “what is the first right thing you need to do?” And if you do that first right thing, you generally can get yourself out of most of those main issues. And so that was really what we went through every time we were faced with a challenge. What’s the first right thing? Let’s go slow and methodical. Let’s try to figure out what’s going on with the vehicle, what’s going on in Houston, and let’s talk through this. And we didn’t have a whole lot of of major emergencies on this on this mission, and everything that we did have pretty much turned out to be a false alarm. So maybe we weren’t challenged sufficiently, but that’s a long answer to a short question.

 

Nilufar Ramji

Oh, please. I think that you were challenged more than enough, and you executed well as a team. And one of the best parts about being a captain on a ship is actually having your shipmates work with you, and that’s the collaboration.

 

Reid Wiseman 

I love my shipmates! Yeah, I love them!

 

Nilufar Ramji 

That’s wonderful. So now everyone has talked about how successful the Artemis II mission was, how much we accomplished, how much we learned, how much we may have increased efficiencies. So tell us something about the Artemis II mission that the public may not know happened.

 

Reid Wiseman

Oh, it was so hard to get to our food. We we had this amazing food. We had like the best space food that any astronauts have ever eaten in space from our food lab here at Johnson Space Center. But it was just it was it was very hard for us to get to the food and the way you would want to eat. They’re just in these large bags, and breakfast is mashed in with lunch, which is mashed in with dinner, and so we had these great menus. But in the end of the day, you’re floating, and we had no way to store all this food that was coming out of this bag. Just like, like think if if you if you pack for a long trip and maybe you use one of those compression pockets where they take all the air out. So three days worth of clothes fits into like a baseball glove, you know, backpack. So think about that, and now think about removing gravity and your weightless. And you take scissors and you cut open that backpack, and now all that force is there, and everything that’s in that backpack now is expanding. Well, think if it’s 10 days worth of food, or in our case, three days worth of food, that’s a tremendous amount of packaging and food products that we just didn’t have anywhere to store. We didn’t have little ziplocks. We didn’t have ways to organize. We didn’t have ways to dig in and find all this food. So we were really behind timeline the first few days as these four humans are in this small vehicle living. It’s a magnificent vehicle, and once we hit our stride towards the end of the mission, it was very easy to work and live in there, and very comfortable. But those first few days, I think the thing that was just getting to me the most was was food, and then maybe a little bit of Outlook inefficiencies based on how we’re using the Deep Space Network with Outlook. Like I joked that with my kids, they would get my response to two days ago’s email today, because we would only get a sync once twice a day. So they would email me. I wouldn’t. I would get that on the next sync. I would reply on the next sync, and then they would get it on the next sync. So that’s like 48 hours from the time they email me until they get anything back. And so think about how out of sorts we were. Like I’m trying to answer questions from Monday, but it’s Wednesday, and then on Friday they’re reading my answers on Wednesday to their Monday email.

 

Nilufar Ramji 

That’s like longer than a comm delay from here to Mars.

 

Reid Wiseman 

By far! So anyway, that I mean that led to some humor, but it’s it was just an honor that we even had Outlook and that we could get email from our families. That’s cool.

 

Nilufar Ramji 

That’s awesome. I keep thinking about the first couple of days of the mission when a hazelnut spread floated across.

 

Reid Wiseman 

Are we not allowed to say Nutella? Yep, we took that. You know, NASA. I love them because they’ll take the labels off of water bottles in press conferences, but somehow no one took the label off that that bottle of Nutella.

 

Nilufar Ramji 

I keep thinking about your food going everywhere, and that was one of the items of food that went somewhere!

 

Reid Wiseman 

That was that was one of the first things we unpacked. It was in our flight day zero food kit, and I do love a good dessert.

 

Nilufar Ramji

I think we all have a sweet tooth.

So tell us one thing that you may have worried about that the rest of the team it may not have been in the back of their minds. Was there some a particular thing that you maybe as commander as a as the fourth crew member…

 

Reid Wiseman 

I love the way you just said that. I think people have asked me if I was scared on this mission, and and the short answer is yes, absolutely. There is you’re 250,000 miles away from home. There’s- if you don’t have a little bit of fear underneath the surface. I think something’s wrong with you, and I definitely felt that. I felt my heart rate go up a few times. I could tell I was… anxious isn’t the right word. I was comfortable, but I could tell it’s just the position that you’re in is it is one that is fear invoking. But I think the thing that maybe I was most worried about was, and it’s a false worry in hindsight, but just crew cohesion on this, and the stress of the situation. And I was not, I I was not adding to the crew cohesion. I mean, I’m a, I got all all the same faults that everybody else does, but I think that was the one thing. Like, I just want the four of us to stay on the rails, and keep this thing going through the mission. And I mean, goodness, we did.

Like, oh, I mean, just couldn’t believe it. Even, even when we splashed down to the Pacific Ocean, the first thing that Victor said to me was, “Now I’m excited.” Because he had said the whole time, like, “I’m not going to be excited until we splash down.” And just the fact that we splashed out, we just gone from 39 times the speed of sound, we splashed out the Pacific Ocean, and he looks over at me and goes, “Now I’m excited.” I mean, all that stuff was just amazing. It was just gems, and Christina was doing it. Jeremy was doing it. I was doing it. Like we just all had these gems.

A minute and a half into flight, Jeremy, we’re still on on powered flight, Jeremy just goes, “This is so cool.”

 

Nilufar Ramji 

It is!

 

Reid Wiseman 

And when we lifted off off the pad, the first thing that Christina said was “poyekhali” [поехали], which is what we say in Russia when we launch on a Soyuz. It’s like a long tradition, “we are going” kind of of thing. It just the the fact that the crew was on this elevated level of situational awareness and comradeship the whole time. It was just, it was just, it was truly surreal. It was. I just can’t believe how that all played out.

 

Nilufar Ramji 

There’s so much job love that you’re, you’re exemplifying right now

 

Reid Wiseman 

Moon Joy, job love, all of it!

 

Nilufar Ramji 

All of it. Okay, so let’s go back in time. Let’s go back to 2009, and if today’s Reid could spend five minutes with the Reid who had just been selected as an astronaut, is there a particular piece of advice you would give him?

 

Reid Wiseman 

Well, when we when we take this question all the way back to like teenage years and youth, I always say, “Dude, chill out.” That’s what I would tell me.

I think this this might be an odd answer, but especially coming from the military, you move around a lot. You know, you’re always moving jobs. There’s, people are always coming and going, and you get super attached in the military because you’re just in this highly operational environment. You have to be attached to everybody. But those people that you go to attached to, they leave quick. In the astronaut office in 2009, I didn’t realize at that time, I should have, but I didn’t realize that I would grow old with my peers now. I would grow old with my fellow astronauts. I I just went to the wedding of a daughter of a fellow astronaut. I mean, our kids are getting married. I’ve watched us all get wrinkles and gray hair, and you know we press through life together. And I think if I could go back in ’09, I would just tell myself like it’s it’s more important to know the names of the kids of your classmates than it is to ace a test. I think that’s the advice I would give myself.

 

Nilufar Ramji

That’s really good advice. I like that. It’s very humble. So you talked about switching many jobs. You’ve had a couple of lives. You were a fighter pilot, a test pilot, an astronaut, Chief Astronaut, named commander. So, which of these roles, these titles, changed you the most as a person?

 

Reid Wiseman

Oh, I was going to totally bridge that question into my daughters Ellie and Katey, but I will honor your question for sure. Being the Chief Astronaut changed me the most. That was where Brené Brown came and talked to us. She’s I don’t know what you would call her, like a mentor, a self-help philosopher, and I’ve heard this phrase before, but it really hit home with her, which is “you got to be in the arena.”

I was at a at a softball game for my kid, when they were really little, and there was a big metal sign on the backstop, and it said, “Have a complaint?” Question mark. “Have you volunteered?” Question mark. That’s kind of the same thing to me with being in the arena. Is, the chief astronaut, I have never served in a position that is more thankless than that job. Every decision you make, you’re leading 50 peers, accomplished humans. Many of them were older than me. They were more experienced than me. They were far smarter than me, and I’m just trying to do my best every day, just pedaling as hard as I can to make the right decision for the program and for the astronaut office. But even more importantly, for the astronaut. And sometimes the right decision for the astronaut, from their eyes is definitely wrong. But as the chief, you’re trying to look and go, “I know this is wrong for you from your eyes, but this is right for you from the umbrella.” And that’s a horrible thing to have to try to navigate. But that is how I tried to navigate that stuff. So I developed more when I was the chief in that in that two years than in in any other job, it is it is just a very challenging job.

 

Nilufar Ramji 

Sounds like it stretched you in ways that you didn’t think you could be stretched.

 

Reid Wiseman 

Yes.

 

Nilufar Ramji 

And I did, just going back to my last question, as I said the question to you or asked it to you, I thought husband, father… So many titles. So I want to take a moment to acknowledge your family because as much as you contributed to this mission, your family did a lot to support you and be your champion. To give you to us to be able to make this happen.

 

Reid Wiseman 

I want to tell one story on that. So thank you for that. I mean, my two daughters, they definitely showed up for this mission, which was which was almost, I would say surprising. But the one story I wanted to tell you is we were on the seventh day of the mission, and I was doing a video chat with them. And my older daughter really didn’t want me to fly this mission. You start, or I started to realize, wow, I’ve been really lucky in my 300 days here that nothing has gone wrong. And you start to wonder, and I’ve talked to like you know we have these performance psychologists, and they said this is a very common feeling for people that are doing like their last combat run in a deployment, where you start to think, oh no, I’ve got this one more thing to do. Is my luck going to run out on this? And to, you know, have gone through both the deconditioning of zero gravity, despite all the exercising that we do every day on station, and then coming up against literally only two weeks left to my mission and having to kind of ramp back up to maximum concentration, performance, mental and physical fortitude to do a series of spacewalks. That was that was something I had to dig deep for, for sure.

 

Nilufar Ramji

That’s definitely something that you want to grow into, and I’m glad that you had the time to do that. And you spent you talked about the number of days you spent in space, and you’ve also been so far away from Earth. Has that changed your definition of home?

 

Christina Koch

I have done a lot of adventures and experiences away from home, and I think that the important thing that it’s taught me is that home is really your loved ones.

 

Nilufar Ramji 

Yes.

 

Christina Koch 

And it’s just as simple as that. I have definitely been someone that could have been described as like an experience chaser, but what I’ve learned very early on is that you should be a relationships chaser, and that the relationships you have both with your loved ones and new people that you meet are far more important than any experiences that you’re getting under your belt just to see a new place.

 

Nilufar Ramji

I completely agree with you on that. I want you to take us home when you think about all of the young people who watched you on this historic flight around the Moon, and they thought maybe one day I could do that too. What’s your hope for them, and what would you like to say to them?

 

Christina Koch

Well, my hope for them is that they get to do everything that gives them fulfillment. I used to say find your passion, and more recently, I have changed kind of how I view that into find your fulfillment. And find something you can actually do the slowest for the longest and still truly love it. And so I think that’s what I would hope for people chasing their dreams, maybe at the beginning of their career, or maybe just in the very end and wanting to chase a new dream. The thing that gives you satisfaction and meaning, not necessarily because it’s what everyone else says is an achievement or success, but because it truly makes you feel wonderful, and it’s not a flash in the pan passion that you’re going to achieve and then drop. It’s something that you would literally do forever if you could. That’s what I hope for them.

And how do you get there? You do the things that scare you, you don’t go away from the direction of things that you think you might not be able to do. You go toward that direction because, in the end, that’s how you contribute most to the world, and that’s how you get the most satisfaction out of achieving things. Is when you’ve done something you didn’t even think that you could.

And the last thing I would leave them with is supporting the people around them. When we think about lifting up everyone, that’s when I have found that, as a collateral, collateral thing from focusing on that, you also are lifted up. But again, the most good for the whole world is achieved. And you also can have some pretty great insights about your own fulfillment when you stop thinking about it so hard and you start trying to make sure that everyone around you is also fulfilled.
