1        Executive Summary
This work examines how microgravity conditions on spacecraft change the development of smoke
aerosol relative to smoke on Earth. We perform simulations of particle formation and growth with
the goal of capturing smoke dynamics similar to those realized in experiments performed aboard the
International Space Station. The simulations provide detailed information about fluid/thermal/particle
interactions that cannot be acquired experimentally on orbit. A surrogate material, dibutyl pthalate, is
expelled from a hot surface in a rectangular chamber while ambient air is blown through the domain,
convecting the developing smoke downstream. The study considers two flow rates, two geometric con-
figurations, and the isolated effects of gravitational particle settling and thermophoresis. In Earth gravity,
buoyancy produces asymmetries in all fields of interest and amplifies vapor-to-particle conversion near
room temperature surfaces at the upper boundary of the simulation domain. Gravitational settling re-
moves large particles from the aerosol, limiting the maximum particle size to ∼ 40 µm and the mode
particle size to ∼ 5 µm. Without buoyancy or settling, the microgravity smoke plume is spatially symmet-
ric and contains very large particles (> 40 µm). A bimodal particle size distribution results in microgravity
when prominent recirculation occurs behind the hot vapor source. The distribution is comprised of large
particles (> 20 µm) associated with long residency time, vapor rich, high temperature recirculation, and
smaller particles (< 10 µm) that originate farther downstream of the hot vapor source. A distinct large
particle mode does not appear in microgravity flows that do not have prominent high temperature re-
circulation, although the aerosol still contains large particles. Without recirculation, the mode particle
diameter in microgravity is ∼ 10 µm, or approximately twice that of terrestrial smoke. Results show that
future experiments should carefully consider and quantify surface interactions and the presence of hot,
recirculating vapor, in order to control geometry-dominated features of the particle size distribution.
A manuscript based on this work will be submitted to the journal Aerosol Science & Technology. The
final document, when accepted, will be provided to NASA.

2        Introduction
Gravity affects the evolution of aerosols by introducing buoyancy and particle settling. Buoyant convec-
tion modifies the time-temperature history of the aerosol, as well as the spatial distribution of particles,
condensing vapor, and thermal energy. Gravity also causes the settling of large particles out of the
aerosol, provided the chemistry and vapor concentrations allow for the generation of large particles
with appreciable settling velocities. These factors combine to increase particle residency times in high
temperature, vapor rich regions in microgravity systems relative to terrestrial or Earth gravity systems
(Urban et al., 1998, 2005). As a result, smoke particles produced in microgravity tend to be larger than
those produced on Earth (Ku et al., 1995, Urban et al., 1998). Measurements suggest microgravity
smoke particles to be 2 − 8 times larger than terrestrial smoke particles (Urban et al., 2005). Moreover,
large particles (not only smoke particles) persist in microgravity, with mass concentrations of particles
with diameter > 100 µm measured at ten-times that of a nominal terrestrial environment (Liu et al.,
1991). Total particulate mass concentrations are also more than five times greater in microgravity (Liu
et al., 1991). Understanding gravitational effects is of practical interest because it assists in the design
of mission-critical smoke detection systems for use in microgravity environments, including the Interna-
tional Space Station (Mulholland et al., 2015); systems developed and calibrated for terrestrial use may
not perform correctly in microgravity because of inappropriate particle size sensitivities (Urban et al.,
2005).
The present work uses numerical simulation to explore the influence of gravity on pyrolysis smoke
growth from a fundamental perspective. We simulate the formation and growth of dibutyl-phthalate
(DBP) particles in Earth gravity and microgravity environments. We utilize direct numerical simulation
via a Navier-Stokes nodal approach. The smoke originates from pyrolysis vapor expelled from a heated
sample in a geometry similar to that considered in the SAME experiments (Mulholland et al., 2015). The
intention of this effort is to quantify the differences between microgravity and Earth gravity smoke, and
to identify the fluid dynamic mechanisms that cause the differences. This study considers two cross-
flow velocities of sufficiently low speed that buoyancy cannot be neglected in the presence of gravity.
Simulations are presented to assess the dependence of particle size distributions on thermophoresis,
particle settling, and flow geometry. The paper is organized as follows: we introduce the simulation and
modeling methodology, followed by a description of the flow geometry and the parameter space under
consideration; we then examine differences in the wake geometry attributable to buoyancy for the lower
of two flow speeds; next, we present and discuss instantaneous scalar fields as a means to describe
particle accumulation and growth in the buoyant and non-buoyant wakes that form downstream of the
hot vapor source; after developing an understanding of how the particle fields interact with the fluid
dynamics for the low speed cases, we consider the effects of thermophoresis, particle settling, flow
geometry, and the higher flow speed; we conclude with a discussion of the results, summarize findings
relevant to future experimentation, and outline directions for future work.

4.1     FLOW CONFIGURATION
We simulate the formation and growth of DBP particles in air flowing over a heated sample that expels
DBP vapor. The simulation mimics the smoldering or pre-conflagration stage of a fire studied in experi-
ments performed aboard the ISS (Mulholland et al., 2015). A schematic of the computational domain is
shown in Fig. 1. Room temperature air enters a long chamber and flows over a heated rectangular sam-
ple. Vapor released from the sample nucleates into particles that grow and are subsequently collected
downstream for analysis. The flow is laminar and steady, allowing the two-dimensional representation
to present physical characteristics largely representative of the SAME experiments (Mulholland et al.,
2015). DBP is used as the surrogate material because its properties are known and its use in modeling
is well-established (unlike the materials used in the SAME experiments).


4.2     PHYSICAL CONDITIONS
Constant fluid properties for air and DBP are provided in Table 1, and variable DBP properties are
provided in Table 2. The enhancement factor required for the nucleation expression, Eqn. (20), is
defined for DBP as cnuc = 3.2 × 10−4 (Nguyen et al., 1987, Pyykönen and Jokiniemi, 2000).
The thickness of the heated DBP source serves as the reference length scale, Lo = 0.0044 m (illustrated
in Fig. 1), which is representative of the length scale in the SAME experiments (Mulholland et al.,
2015). The air enters from the left with velocity Uo and temperature To = 300 K. We consider two
inlet velocities: Uo = 0.08 m/s, the velocity considered in the SAME experiments, and a slightly faster
flow of Uo = 0.10 m/s. The upper and lower boundaries are impermeable, no-slip, iso-thermal surfaces
(Twall = To ). The heated rectangle is also no-slip, has a fixed temperature of Ts = 473.2 K, and releases
DBP vapor at a constant mass flux of q 00 = 8.4 × 10−5 kg/m2 · s. The mass flux has been selected to be
representative of the mass loss data presented by Mulholland et al. (2015). Zero-derivative conditions
are imposed for all species and particle fields at the inlet and outlet. The gravitational acceleration is
considered at terrestrial (go = 9.81 m/s2 ) and microgravity (go = 0 m/s2 ) conditions.
The inlet velocities and the gravitational accelerations considered represent four cases with varying
Reynolds and Froude numbers. A summary of the simulations performed at the two flow speeds and
gravitational levels is given in Table 3. The flags “EG” and “MG” identify the “Earth gravity” and “micro-
gravity” conditions, while 1 and 2 identify the Uo = 0.08 m/s and Uo = 0.1 m/s conditions, respectively.
Case 1MG is therefore defined to have an inlet velocity of Uo = 0.08 m/s and go = 0 m/s2 , corre-
sponding to a Reynolds number Re = 22.3 and a Froude number F r = ∞. In addition to the four base
cases listed in Table 3, we examine the aerosol development sensitivity to: (1) particle settling, (2) ther-
mophoresis, and (3) the flow geometry. For this purpose, a series of additional cases, defined in Table
3, are constructed by modifying the setup of Case 1MG and/or Case 1EG. Case 1EG-NS (where NS
stands for “no settling”) is identical to Case 1EG, but the settling mechanism has been neglected. Case
1MG-NT (where NT stands for “no thermophoresis”) is identical to Case 1MG, but the thermophoresis
transport mechanism has been neglected. Cases 1MG-R and 1EG-R (where R stands for “rotated”) are
identical to Cases 1MG and 1EG, except the heated vapor source has been rotated 90◦ . In all cases,
data is collected and presented at a time of t = 7 seconds.


4.3     NUMERICAL SPECIFICATIONS
The domain is non-uniformly discretized by 960 grid points in the x direction and 240 grid point in
the y direction. The governing equations are solved on this computational mesh with a second-order
MacCormack scheme. The particle size space is discretized by 45 particle nodes, with the volume of
each successive node increased by a factor of 2.25, i.e. vk = 2.25 × vk−1 . The volume of the first node
is selected to be the volume of a DBP molecule, v1 = vm = 4.44 × 10−28 m3 . Boundary conditions are

4.4     PHYSICAL ASSUMPTIONS
All assumptions utilized in this work are listed below.
   • From the perspective of thermal conductivity and viscosity, the condensing vapor phase is con-
     sidered sufficiently dilute such that µ and k are computed for air as a function of temperature and
     density (Kadoya et al., 1985). Both the air and the condensing vapor are considered calorically
     perfect gases with constant properties defined in Table 1.
   • The Mach number M is only important for flows with M & 0.3. Although the physics are insen-
     sitive to variation in Mach number when the Mach number is small, the ratio of sound speed to
     convective speed imposes a large computational burden. The computational challenge is often
     remedied with a Boussinesq approximation, but this approach is not valid in the presence of large
     density variations. Additionally, an elliptical solve for pressure is computationally intractable for the
     number of iterations required to resolve the particle time scales. We instead use a scaled dynamic
                                                             2
     pressure as the reference pressure Po = ρo (c1 Uo ) , such that P ? = ραPRoα T , where c1 is specified
     to reduce the effective speed of sound to 8 m/s. The flow field was found to be independent of c1
     for the values used.
   • All particles are assumed to be spherical.
   • Condensed phase DBP properties are defined by their continuum values.
   • There is no momentum exchange between the particles and the fluid (the air/DBP vapor mixture);
     the particle velocity equals the fluid velocity, augmented by the thermophoretic velocity and the
     stokes settling velocity. The stokes settling velocity is assumed to apply for all particles in the
     domain (including the large particles).
   • The fluid and particles are in thermal equilibrium and have the same temperature.
   • Particle diffusion coefficients decrease rapidly with diameter. This poses a numerical challenge
     due to the lack of a physical mechanism for diffusing numerical errors. For this reason, the mini-
     mum diffusion coefficient considered in the particle mass fraction transport equations corresponds
     to the fifth particle size node – any node greater than the fifth node is assigned the particle diffu-
     sion coefficient of the fifth node.
   • The mass flux of DPB from the heated rectangle is spatially uniform and all surfaces have spatially
     uniform temperature.
   • Particles that impact a solid surface “stick” with 100% efficiency, and are removed from the flow.
   • The aerosol does not achieve a rigorous steady-state because particle evolution continues (slowly)
     in regions of the flow with recirculation, particularly behind the hot vapor source. The recirculation
     is characterized by high temperatures, long particle residency times, and the continuous intro-
     duction of additional vapor. The hydrodynamic and thermal fields, conversely, become steady
     after only a few seconds. Although a rigorous steady-state does not exist, all aerosol features
     develop and effectively stabilize shortly after the hydrodynamic fields. We therefore present all
     results at time t = 7 seconds, which is considered to be the effective steady-state. At his time, all
     fields are either steady, or changing at a rate that does not affect the conclusions drawn from the
     simulations.

4.5     FLUID FLOW, TEMPERATURE, AND MASS FRACTION
The velocity field controls the convection of thermal energy, condensible species vapor, and particles.
Vorticity ω, defined as the curl of the velocity vector

                                                ω = ∇ × u,                                              (31)

is positive in regions of the flow where fluid rotates counterclockwise, and is negative in regions where
the fluid rotates clockwise. The sign and magnitude of vorticity therefore identifies the location, orienta-
tion, and intensity of vortices and boundary layers.
Figure 2 shows the steady dimensionless vorticity. Panel (a) shows Case 1MG and panel (b) shows
Case 1EG. The grey rectangles represent the heated vapor source. Red fluid rotates counterclockwise
(positive vorticity) and blue fluid rotates clockwise (negative vorticity). Immediately upon entering the
channel, the fluid at the walls is brought to rest by the no slip condition, signified by the large vorticity
gradients in the upper and lower left corners of panels (a) and (b). As the fluid travels downstream
from left to right, boundary layers develop symmetrically on the upper and lower walls due to viscous
diffusion. Further downstream, the heated rectangle reduces the cross-sectional flow area, and the
pressure field accelerates the fluid through flow paths above and below the heated vapor source. The
microgravity case in panel (a) remains symmetric near the heated vapor source, whereas the Earth
gravity case in panel (b) develops an asymmetry with the majority of fluid passing below the heated
vapor source (indicated by larger vorticity magnitudes below than above). Comparatively low vorticity
values on the downstream side of the rectangles indicate the presence of low speed, recirculating
wakes. The microgravity wake in panel (a) is symmetric, whereas the Earth gravity wake in panel (b) is
asymmetric. As the fluid continues to flow downstream, the boundary layers thicken in both cases and
the fluid exits the domain to the right. Variations in density due to heat transfer from the rectangle have
influenced the vorticity fields via baroclinic torque and buoyancy. Because there is no gravity in Case

5        Summary
We have simulated smoke formation and growth in microgravity and Earth gravity to examine how
buoyancy and particle settling affect thermal/fluid/particle interactions and the resultant smoke particle
size distributions. The simulated geometry emulates the SAME experiments performed aboard the
International Space Station (Mulholland et al., 2015). Dibutyl pthalate (DBP) vapor is released from a
heated rectangular sample, which serves as a surrogate for the smoldering material used in the SAME
experiments.
Large particles (> 30 µm) are present in the wake region downstream of the heated vapor source, and
large particle number densities (∼ 1010 m−3 ) occur inside the wake vortices. The wake contains large
particle number densities because dividing streamlines prevent the particles from being convected away,
and the particles continue to grow as condensation consumes all available DBP vapor. The particles
simultaneously grow via coagulation. Downstream of the wake, two parallel particle streams contain
high number densities of both large and small particles. No particles are present far downstream with
diameter < 400 nm because nucleation only occurs for a short distance downstream of the heated va-
por source. The particle size distributions contain local maximums near dp = 5 µm and dp = 35 µm for
both flow speeds. The faster flow conditions present a slightly larger wake but this does not manifest
as a meaningful difference in particle size distribution. Thermophoresis prevents particles from contact-
ing the heated surface, which has been demonstrated by turning off the thermophoresis mechanism.
Without thermophoresis, particles contact the heated vapor source and undergo evaporation. With re-
spect to the particle size distribution, this has the effect of decreasing the number of large particles and
increasing the number of small particles – this supports an assertion that the wake is responsible for
producing the largest particles in the system.
When gravity is present, all fields become asymmetric due to buoyant convection. Upward convection
in the faster flow brings DBP vapor into close proximity with the cool upper surface, resulting in locally
high nucleation rates near the upper wake vortex and the upper surface. Large particle number densi-
ties (∼ 1011 m−3 ) are present along the upper surface, and slightly lower number densities are present
in the upper wake vortex. Comparably few particles are present in the lower wake vortex, where the
diameter of average mass is maximized. Downstream of the wake vortices, gravitational settling elimi-
nates all particles with diameter > 35 µm. Unlike the microgravity case, the 8 cm/s Earth gravity case is
not bimodal, with a single local maximum at dp = 5 µm. Without particle settling, a bimodal distribution
results with two local maximums at particle diameters comparable to the microgravity case. Rotating the
heated vapor source 90◦ dramatically reduces the wake volume, and no particles appear in the small
wake. Rotating the heated vapor source does not affect the Earth gravity case as significantly as the
microgravity case. Increasing the flow speed from decreases the prominence of buoyancy, delays inter-
action with the upper surface until further downstream, widens the particle size distribution to include
particles on the nanometer scale, and introduces a second local maximum to the PSD near dp = 30 µm.
Microgravity smoke presented in this study contains larger particles than terrestrial smoke. When the
microgravity PSD is bimodal, the larger mode contains particles with dp > 35 µm, while the terrestrial
PSD contains effectively zero particles in this size range. Alternatively, the microgravity PSD can be uni-
modal with a mode diameter of dp = 10 µm – roughly twice that of the terrestrial case. Results exhibit
three primary mechanisms that differentiate the microgravity and Earth gravity smoke: (1) different re-
circulation geometries and volumes modify the time-temperature-concentration history of the aerosol in
regions of high temperature and high vapor mass fraction; (2) particle settling eliminates large particles
from Earth gravity smoke, which reduces the maximum particle size and modifies aerosol development
by changing coagulation and condensation rates of the remaining particles; (3) buoyant convection can
transport condensible species toward relatively cool surfaces, increasing vapor-to-particle conversion.
Each of these factors tend to increase the size of particles in microgravity relative to Earth gravity. How-
ever, each of these factors is also highly material- and geometry-dependent: (1) the wake dynamic
becomes unimportant if nucleation occurs far downstream of the wake, or if there is a time dependent
flow without a stable recirculation region; (2) particle settling is irrelevant if the condensing species forms
particles that are too small to settle (the DBP considered in this work forms large particles); (3) if smoke
is produced in an open environment, there may not be cool surfaces nearby to increase nucleation
rates. In these situations, microgravity and Earth gravity smoke are hypothesized to be more similar. In
