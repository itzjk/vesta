Structure & Liftoff In Combustion Experiment (SLICE)
SLICE Impacts, Benefits, and Products
       Compiled by Dennis Stocker, SLICE Project Scientist and Co-Investigator
             216-433-2166, dennis.p.stocker@nasa.gov


Structure & Liftoff In Combustion Experiment (SLICE) is a precursor to the Coflow Laminar
Diffusion Flame (CLD Flame) experiment, where the SLICE results will be used to maximize the
scientific return of that upcoming space station experiment. CLD Flame is one of five
experiments in the Advanced Combustion via Microgravity Experiments (ACME) project that are
currently in development for conduct in ISS’ Combustion Integrated Rack (CIR). It is currently
expected that the ACME experiments will be conducted from 2016 to 2019, whereas SLICE was
conducted in early 2012 in the Microgravity Science Glovebox (MSG). A common goal of SLICE
and CLD Flame is to improve computational techniques such that a broader range of flame
conditions can be effectively modeled than had been possible.

The twin purposes for the SLICE research are increased fuel efficiency and reduced pollutant
emission in practical combustion devices on Earth. More explicitly, the experiment was
conducted to advance combustion modeling capability, which “allows system designers to
improve efficiency and reduce harmful pollutants in ways never before possible” (as stated by
an external reviewer of the SLICE experiment). In other words, the SLICE research was
conducted to advance energy technology and improve our global environment. It is also
plausible that SLICE results could benefit one or more space exploration technologies, e.g.,
waste processing or spacecraft fire safety, but that was not the intent of the research.

SLICE has provided a unique set of data to validate coflow diffusion flame numerical models,
such as that of SLICE Co-Investigator Mitchell Smooke et al., described for example in SLICE
papers [1-2]. From [1], “… the MC-Smooth vorticity-velocity formulation was employed to
describe the reactive gaseous mixture, and soot evolution was modeled by sectional aerosol
equations. The governing equations and boundary conditions were discretized on a two-
dimensional computational domain by finite differences, and the resulting set of fully coupled,
strongly nonlinear equations was solved simultaneously at all points using a damped, modified
Newton's method.”

By comparing numerical predictions with corresponding experimental measurements, the
numerical model and submodels (e.g., thermal radiation, soot formation, chemical mechanisms)
can be evaluated and validated over a much broader range of flame conditions than would be
available under normal-gravity conditions. Validated by SLICE measurements, the numerical
model provides information that was not measured experimentally, e.g., the velocity field, heat
release rate, mixture fraction, and consumption rate of the individual species. This information
can help improve our understanding of the complicated effects of buoyancy on flame structure
and the interactions between the flow field and chemical reactions. The novel SLICE data also
serves as a benchmark to optimize the computations. While simulating CH4 flames, the SLICE
researchers have improved the predictive capability of the numerical model through optimization
of its boundary conditions and evaluation of the performance of several kinetic reaction
mechanisms. Furthermore, in conjunction with the SLICE data, the numerical model is being
tested over a large set of parameter ranges, where this effort is expected to help generate more

reliable predictions for ACME’s CLD Flame experiment. This improved capability can help
researchers optimize the CLD Flame test matrix and thus maximize its scientific return.

While soot pyrometry was successfully accomplished in the SLICE experiment, the experiment
led to refined pyrometry requirements for the five ACME experiments. More specifically, SLICE
led to a new specification for the BG-7 filter transmission, where that filter will be important for
equalizing the intensity in all three channels (i.e., red, blue, and green) of the Gigabit Ethernet
(GigE) camera, which will prevent data loss (from saturation) and improve the data quality (e.g.,
signal to noise ratio). This is important for both soot pyrometry and Thin Filament Pyrometry
(TFP), where those are ACME’s primary techniques to measure the temperature field.

In addition, the results of the SLICE experiment will help enable optimization of the camera
settings for the ACME experiments. SLICE provided preliminary results on the soot volume
fraction, which (along with the flame intensity) is often much greater in microgravity than normal
gravity. Those results combined with a quantitative comparison of the sensitivities of the SLICE
and ACME cameras (accounting for the associated optics and optical layouts) will guide the
selection of appropriate ACME camera settings.

The CLD Flame test matrix is also being reviewed in light of the SLICE results, where that
process could lead to refinement of the test matrix so as to optimize CLD Flame’s scientific
return.
