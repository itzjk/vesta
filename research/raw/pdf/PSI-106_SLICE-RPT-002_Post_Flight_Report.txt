                                         PREFACE

The Structure & Liftoff In Combustion Experiment (SLICE) is a precursor to the Coflow
Laminar Diffusion Flame (CLD Flame) experiment, which is one of the four ACME (Advanced
Combustion via Microgravity Experiments) investigations that are currently in development for
conduct within the Combustion Integrated Rack (CIR). The goal of the experiment is to
investigate the structure of lifting and lifted flames, where the flame detaches from the nozzle
and stabilizes at a downstream position. The results will be used to minimize risk and maximize
the scientific return of the CLD Flame Experiment. The SLICE investigation was conducted in
the Microgravity Science Glovebox (MSG) using the Smoke Point In Coflow Experiment
(SPICE) hardware.

1.0    INTRODUCTION
SLICE, the Structure & Liftoff In Combustion Experiment, is a space station investigation of the
structure of lifting and lifted flames, where flow conditions and chemistry cause them to detach
and stabilize downstream of the burner.

SLICE is a crew-operated combustion experiment that will be conducted within the Microgravity
Science Glovebox (MSG) on the International Space Station (ISS). A flame of gaseous fuel, such
as methane which is the primary component of natural gas, will be generated within a small fan-
driven flow duct. With guidance from the ground team, the astronaut will vary the fuel or air
flow and take still photographs in parallel with video photography. The calibrated still
photography will enable determination of the soot temperature and volume fraction.

1.1    Purpose
The data will be used to improve modeling capability with the overall goal of increased fuel
efficiency and reduced pollutant emission in practical combustion devices here on Earth. SLICE
is a precursor to the Coflow Laminar Diffusion Flame (CLD Flame) experiment which is in
development for ISS’ Combustion Integrated Rack (CIR) as part of the Advanced Combustion
via Microgravity Experiments (ACME) project.

1.2    Scope
SLICE is a joint effort of Yale University, the National Center for Space Exploration Research
(NCSER), NASA, and ZIN Technologies, Inc. The experiment is managed at the NASA Glenn
Research Center (in Cleveland, OH), which is NASA’s lead for microgravity combustion
research. If you are looking for more information about SLICE, please visit:
http://issresearchproject.grc.nasa.gov/MSG/SLICE/

3.0    SLICE SCIENCE OVERVIEW
3.1    Background
Soot formation in hydrocarbon-fueled flames is an important unresolved problem of combustion
science that affects the durability and performance of power and propulsion systems, the hazards
of unwanted fires, the pollutant emissions of combustors and the potential for developing
computational combustion.

Coflow laminar diffusion flames are especially valuable for studies of combustion because of the
availability of accurate numerical modeling with that flame configuration. In particular, excellent
agreement can be achieved when the flow conditions are such that the flame detaches and lifts
above (i.e., moves downstream of) the nozzle. A coupled experimental and numerical
investigation can enable validation and improvements to combustion modeling. Enhanced
modeling capability is important because it can reduce time and cost in the design of practical
combustion devices. Furthermore, flame attachment to (or detachment from) a burner or
condensed-fuel surface is of essential importance in both combustion systems and fire safety.
The flame attachment point controls the stability of the entire trailing diffusion flame.

3.2       Objectives
For diffusion flames of methane, ethylene, and selected nitrogen dilutions of each fuel burning in
a coflow of air:

(1) Characterize the structure of the flame, especially its base (i.e., stabilizing region), from
attached through lifted conditions as a function of the fuel, burner diameter, and flow conditions.

(2) Identify the liftoff velocity limits as a function of the fuel and burner diameter.

This will enable refinement of the flame diagnostic settings and test matrix for the CLD Flame
experiment. It will also allow for a preliminary assessment of the numerical codes’ ability to
predict the flame structure under the broader conditions that are only found in microgravity
flames. The computation of attached flames will require modification of the numerical models to
incorporate burner heat loss.

3.4     Operational Requirments
The standard test procedure was simplistic in nature. The flame was ignited, then, the test
operators relied on voice commands to adjust the fuel flows and air velocity until the flame
extinguished.

3.4.1    Pyrometry Calibration
The fiber is placed into position in the test chamber and a non-smoking flame is established so
that the luminous portion of the flame is just below the fiber. Images are then taken with the still
camera to provide a temperature measurement at the fiber position and an absolute intensity
calibration.

3.5     Success Critera

3.5.1    Minimum Success
Still images are acquired for at least 15 different lifted flames with different combinations of fuel
flow and air velocity with a single fuel, or at least 10 different lifted flames each of two fuels, or
at least 5 different lifted flames each of 3 or more fuels.

3.5.2    High Success
Still images are acquired of at least 30 different lifted flames total from at least 3 different fuels.
The still images are acquired with a blackbody characterized camera for soot pyrometry or liftoff
limits are determined for at least one fuel.

3.5.3    Complete Success
Testing conducted with all 12 fuel bottles, with identification of stability limits and smoke points
(within the range of the SPICE velocity limits). Still images are taken of lifted flames with a
blackbody characterized camera.

Don, thanks for your efforts and skill in capturing images of the lifted flames. The microgravity flames
have lifted at signficantly greater velocities than the normal-gravity flames, but upon lifting have been
much less stable. In the testing on GMT 048, which was with 20% ethylene (diluted with nitrogen), a

of either (1) the glovebox being open (i.e., where the gloveports are not sealed) or (2) the glovebox air
circulation being on. Much higher flow readings are observed when the front door of the SPICE
experiment assembly is open, although that condition can lead to readings that significantly exceed
those from GMT 040. Next week’s testing includes more lifted flame tests with the 100% and 40%
methane (diluted with nitrogen), but there will be a return to smoke point testing in future weeks.

Don, it’s been a pleasure working with you on SPLICE. We are thankful for your interest and dedication
in making the research a success. With the SLICE and SPICE testing on Monday, using respectively
the remaining 70% methane and 100% propane, we got a wide range of data with bracketed sets of still
photos at approx. 28 flow conditions (including the pyrometry calibration). It was interesting to see the
soot in the 70% methane flames, as that dilution wasn’t expected to approach or reach smoking
conditions. Yet with the near absence of buoyant convection in microgravity, the reduced velocities lead
to longer residence times and enhanced soot production. SPLICE testing will presumably come to a
close on Friday (GMT 083) with smoke point tests using 100% ethylene. However, as a reminder,
SLICE is a precursor for an experiment in development for the CIR, so the results will be valuable for
refining that study. And perhaps we’ll be able to get approval for further SPLICE tests, but meanwhile
we have a wealth of results to analyze. Even neglecting the GMT 083 tests, we obtained over 3,000
high-resolution still flame photos (of ~600 flow conditions), not including the scale, dark current, or
pyrometry calibration photos. FYI, the tilt of the lifted flames isn’t too much of an issue for modeling
comparison when the flames are close to the burner, e.g., less than 1 cm per Co-Investigator Mitch
Smooke. And the broad range of conditions in the microgravity flames is helpful in revealing
shortcomings in existing combustion codes for chemical kinetics, e.g., GRI-Mech. We also look forward
to hearing about the progress on our colleagues’ Burning and Suppression of Solids (BASS) experiment.
Thanks and best wishes!
