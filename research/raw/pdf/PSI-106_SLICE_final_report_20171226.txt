   1. Overview
      The Structure & Liftoff In Combustion Experiment (SLICE) experiment was conducted on
      the International Space Station (ISS), in the Microgravity Science Glovebox (MSG), by
      astronaut Don Pettit during Expedition 30 in early 2012; see Figure 1. SLICE
      investigated the structure and stability of laminar, gas-fueled, non-premixed flames in a
      coflow, where the gas-jet diffusion flame was positioned within a fan-driven flow duct.




      Figure 1. Astronaut Don Pettit conducting SPLICE in the Microgravity Science
      Glovebox (MSG) on the International Space Station (ISS) on March 9, 2012 (i.e., GMT
      055), where that day’s testing including both SLICE and SPICE tests. The MSG is
      covered so that the only experiment illumination is from the flame, (1) which Pettit is
      watching through a gap in the stray light cover, and (2) which can also be seen on the
      upper monitor on Pettit’s left.

      In the future, the photograph in Figure 1 and other SLICE photos will be shared at
      www.flickr.com/photos/space-flames.

      The 12 bottles of gaseous fuel were completely consumed during 13 sessions (days) of
      testing.   There were approx. 120 ignitions, with roughly 4,000 high-resolution
      photographs taken at roughly 700 flame conditions (where the photography was
      bracketed, i.e., with multiple photographs taken at a time under a range of settings).

      A fraction of the tests under SLICE were conducted for the Smoke Point In Coflow
      Experiment (SPICE) experiment, which had been previously conducted in 2009.
      Although they had unique objectives, SLICE and SPICE used the same experimental
      hardware (i.e., the SPICE hardware) and nearly identical procedures but they had
      different researchers and science objectives. Together they came to be informally
      referred to (e.g., by Pettit) as SPLICE which could serve as an acronym for Smoke Point
      & Liftoff In Coflow Experiment. Roughly a quarter of the 2012 SPLICE testing was for
      SPICE.

      SLICE is a precursor to the Coflow Laminar Diffusion Flame (CLD Flame) experiment,
      which is part of the Advanced Combustion via Microgravity Experiments (ACME) project
      which is in development for operation in the ISS’ Combustion Integrated Rack (CIR).
      SLICE results are being used to maximize the scientific return of that upcoming space
      station experiment.




   2. Introduction
      a. Objectives
         The goal of the SLICE experiment was to improve our understanding of the physical
         and chemical processes controlling diffusion (i.e., non-premixed) flame structure and
         lifting phenomena (i.e., stabilization) and to provide for rigorous testing of numerical
         models including thermal radiation, soot formation, and detailed chemical kinetics.
         Good agreement between experimental and computational results had been
         demonstrated for lifted flames at moderate flame conditions, but that agreement
         broke down when the fuel was highly diluted or the soot production was high. SLICE
         was a precursor for the CLD Flame experiment, which is one of five experiments in
         the Advanced Combustion via Microgravity Experiments (ACME) project that are
         currently in development for conduct in the Combustion Integrated Rack (CIR). A
         common goal of the two experiments is to improve computational techniques such
         that a broader range of flame conditions can be effectively modeled than is currently
         possible.

         The specific experimental objectives for SLICE were:
            For diffusion flames of methane, ethylene, and selected nitrogen dilutions of each
            fuel burning in a coflow of air:
            (1) Characterize the structure of the flame, especially its base (i.e., stabilizing
                region), from attached through lifted conditions as a function of the fuel,
                burner diameter, and flow conditions.

                                              5
SLICE Final Report (2017)                                                          2017-DEC-26

            (2) Identify the liftoff velocity limits as a function of the fuel and burner diameter.
         A third objective, listed below, was abandoned a year in advance of spaceflight
         operations because it was determined from bench-top testing (for procedure
         development) that the planned fuel quantity was insufficient for this goal.
            (3) Characterize the hysteresis of flame lifting and reattachment (i.e.,
                stabilization) with flow variations as a function of the fuel and burner
                diameter.

      c. Background
         SLICE was the fifth spaceflight experiment to investigate laminar gas-jet diffusion
         (i.e., non-premixed) flames. And like the preceding experiments, the SLICE
         atmosphere consisted of ~21% oxygen (by volume) with a balance of nitrogen.
         Furthermore, the pressure was ~100 kPa in the 5 experiments, with the partial
         exception of the Laminar Soot Processes (LSP) experiment which also included
         testing at lower values. Three of the predecessor experiments were conducted over
         a decade ago on the space shuttle, i.e., the Space Transportation System (STS),
         while the fourth was conducted on ISS, as listed in Table 2. SLICE is especially
         related to two of those experiments, Enclosed Laminar Flames (ELF) and SPICE.

         The four past experiments were focused on gravity’s effects on the flame structure,
         soot and stability limits, and vortex interactions. The LSP and Turbulent Gas-jet
         Diffusion Flame (TGDF) experiments were both conducted in sealed combustion
         chambers, where the flame was in a quiescent atmosphere. However, vortices were
         created in the TGDF experiment by varying the air entrainment through the
         mechanical variation of an iris diameter, where the iris was (1) orthogonal and
         coaxial to the burner and flame, and (2) positioned near the burner tube outlet.
         TGDF was an automated Getaway Special canister (GAS can) experiment that flew
         in the payload bay of the shuttle. In contrast, LSP was carried out in Spacelab and
         Spacehab in 1997 and 2003, respectively. LSP was operated by the crew and
         conducted within the Combustion Module facility, which is the space shuttle
         precursor to the ISS’ Combustion Integrated Rack (CIR).

         In contrast to LSP and TGDF, ELF was conducted within a coflowing atmosphere,
         where the flame was enclosed within a small fan-driven flow duct with a 76.2-mm
         square cross-section. ELF was crew-operated and conducted in a glovebox facility.
         These factors were also true for its successors SPICE and SLICE.

         NASA began conducting spaceflight combustion experiments in glovebox facilities in
         1992 with the United States Microgravity Laboratory 1 (USML-1) mission, i.e., STS-

                                               7
SLICE Final Report (2017)                                                      2017-DEC-26

         50, in which the Middeck Glovebox (MGBX) facility was located in the Spacelab
         module rather than the shuttle middeck. Microgravity flames tend to quench in the
         absence of buoyant convection, which normally transports air to flames, so the
         glovebox combustion experiments have typically incorporated fans to provide forced
         convection since that start. An STS-50 example is the Wire Insulation Flammability
         (WIF) investigation, in which the wire and flame were centered within a fan-driven
         flow duct (i.e., with forced air flow parallel to the wire).

         In 1997, the Enclosed Laminar Flames (ELF) investigation became the first glovebox
         experiment to be conducted in space with gaseous fuel. Like all combustion
         glovebox experiments carried out on the shuttle, ELF was conducted in the MGBX
         facility, which was located in the middeck on its mission, the United States
         Microgravity Payload 4 (USMP-4) mission, i.e., STS-87. The experimental hardware
         was designed to just fit within that facility, where this may be apparent in Figure 2.
         The 33x18x18 cm experiment module and other key hardware is shown in Figure 3.

         The ELF objectives to investigate the stability and structure of coflow laminar
         diffusion flames were very similar to the SLICE objectives. ELF is very much a
         precursor to SLICE in terms of both hardware and science, even though experiments
         were separated by 15 years and their research teams were different. However,
         Dennis Stocker was an exception in that he served as a Co-I and the PS for both
         experiments.




         Figure 2. Steve Lindsey (left) assists Takao Doi (right) in conducting the Enclosed
         Laminar Flames (ELF) investigation on Nov. 29, 1997 during the USMP-4/STS-87
         space shuttle mission [STS087-E-5088].


         SPICE was initiated as an extension of the LSP experiment to investigate the effect

                                             8
SLICE Final Report (2017)                                                       2017-DEC-26

         of the air coflow on soot processes, i.e., in addition to LSP’s study with flames in a
         quiescent atmosphere. The SPICE hardware was directly based on ELF’s hardware,
         where SPICE was originally planned as a shuttle experiment. The ELF ground
         hardware that had been used for hardware development, crew training, and normal-
         gravity (1g) comparison testing was modified so that it could serve the same purpose
         for SPICE. Although SPICE was planned as a shuttle experiment, the project had a
         convoluted history, e.g., where it was stopped and restarted, such that it was
         ultimately conducted on ISS rather than the shuttle, even though the experiment
         module was designed for the MGBX rather than ISS’s Microgravity Science
         Glovebox (MSG).




         Figure 3. Bench-top photo of Enclosed Laminar Flames (ELF) spaceflight hardware
         and logo [NASA C-97-4013].


         The SPICE hardware included significant improvements as compared to ELF. While
         ELF had a fixed burner tube, SPICE had exchangeable tubes allowing variation of
         the burner diameter. In ELF, the numerical data was physically displayed on the
         front of the module and was photographed along with the flame using a video
         camera. Meanwhile in SPICE, the data was still recorded with the flame video, but it
         was overlaid electronically eliminating the need for the illuminated displays, as can
         be seen in Figure 4 (i.e., where there are no physical displays). ELF used a second
         analog video camera to photograph the flame through the top window, while SPICE
         used a high-resolution digital SLR camera for the top-window photography. SPICE
         had a retracting scale for photography that could be positioned at the flame axis,
         whereas ELF’s reference scale was on the top of the module, i.e., at a significant
         distance from the flame axis. However, SPICE eliminated ELF’s retracting array of
         thermocouples and thin-filament fibers (for pyrometry) to include its retracting scale.
         SPICE also used larger fuel bottles because the MSG working volume was

         used. Other differences are minor in comparison.

         The principal element of the hardware is the SPICE experiment module, which
         mounted to the floor of the MSG’s working volume, where a bench-top photograph is
         seen in Figure 4. The bottom (i.e., ‘basement’) of the module includes a delivery
         system for the gaseous fuel, including a quick connect (for attaching a fuel bottle),
         particulate filter, pressure regulator, and mass flow controller (for 500 sccm on a N2
         basis), where these components can be seen with a bottle connected in Figure 9.

         The upper portion of the experiment module consists of the fan-driven flow duct in
         which the burner tube is located, and where there are top and front windows for
         flame photography. The front window is hinged and opens to allow manual
         exchange of the burner tube and hot-wire igniter, where there are interlocks to
         prevent fuel flow and igniter actuation when it is open. As seen from the front, the

         SPICE’s power/video box assembly is mounted to the back wall of the MSG’s
         working volume. It consists of a power box and video box with a common mounting
         plate. The unit provides for power conversion and distribution, conversion of the
         video from a composite to S-video format, and the titler overlay of the numerical data
         onto the video. The overlay includes the date and time, fuel flow, fan voltage, air
         speed, and radiometer output.

         The SPICE control box was located outside of the MSG through the use of a feed-
         through connector. It allowed the crew to enable or disable the fuel flow and igniter,
         and adjust the fuel flow rate, fan (i.e., coflow) speed, and radiometer gain. The
         assembly, seen in Figure 14, also included a shutter release button for use with the
         digital SLR camera.




         Figure 14. SPICE control box assembly, including the shutter release button for the
         digital SLR camera.

         The 12 SPICE fuel bottles were refilled to support SLICE and the 2012 SPICE
         operations, where example photographs can be seen in Figure 15. The bottles
         simply include a hand valve and quick connect for mating the bottle to the
         experiment module. The bottles were filled with the fuels listed in Table 4, where the
         propane bottle was filled to lower pressure to avoid condensation of the fuel.

         Several hardware changes were made for SLICE, just as SPICE improved upon the
         earlier ELF investigation. However, they were generally relatively minor in scope
         given that the SPICE hardware was mostly already on orbit and because SLICE was
         planned as an inexpensive experiment that would be quickly ready for spaceflight. A
         simple example was the addition of two more burner tube sizes, with inner diameters
         of 2.1 and 3.2 mm. The measured diameters of all of the burner tubes are listed in
         Table 5, to a resolution of 0.0064 mm, where it is a critical measurement because of
         its strong (inverse square) influence on velocity of the fuel jet (for a fixed fuel flow).
         Note that the burner tubes have constant inner diameters even though they have

      e. Procedure
         The SLICE experiment was conducted manually by astronaut Don Pettit in early
         2012. Using the simple hardware, Pettit ignited the flames, adjusted the fuel and air
         flow, and took still photographs of the flame per verbal directions from Dennis
         Stocker via space-to-ground communication. The direct communication (without
         Payload Communication intermediaries) during those flame operations was
         established based on the SLICE crew training and the 2009 experience with Smoke
         Point In Coflow Experiment (SPICE). Although their objectives and research teams
         differed, SLICE and SPICE were sister experiments, where SLICE was conducted
         with the SPICE hardware, although with some additional and upgraded components.
         Both experiments were conducted within the Microgravity Science Glovebox (MSG)
         on the International Space Station (ISS).

         Before each round of testing, Pettit installed the specified burner tube and a supply
         bottle with the selected fuel. He then adjusted the fuel flow and air velocity as
         directed and ignited the flame. Several flame conditions were studied in each test;
         Petitt adjusted either the fuel flow or airflow to achieve different flame conditions.
         When directed, he photographed the flame with the high-resolution digital still
         camera. The fuel flow was shut off upon completion of a test, e.g., which was
         typically when the flame quenched because of the flow conditions. Throughout the
         operations, the ground team monitored the video downlink, which includes overlaid
         sensor data, to guide the crewmember in the testing. The operations were directed
         from the Telescience Support Center (TSC) at NASA Glenn, to which Prof. Long and
         his graduate student Bin Ma were connected via teleconferencing. Arrangements


                                            19
SLICE Final Report (2017)                                                          2017-DEC-26

         were made so that they had access to the video downlink at Yale. Following the
         testing, the still images were downlinked to ground for analysis.

         While it hadn’t been expected or planned, a second camera view was provided in the
         downlink with an over-the-shoulder view of Pettit conducting the experiment. At the
         time, there were only four video channels downlinked from ISS, where SLICE used
         two during the science (i.e., flame) portion of its operations. This and the procedures
         in general can be seen in composite video at:
         http://www.nasa.gov/mission_pages/station/research/news/burming_questions.html
         To show the operations, the experiment (i.e., flame) video was combined with the
         over-the-shoulder video and the space-to-ground communication (i.e., audio)
         between Pettit and SLICE PS/Co-I Dennis Stocker.

         A detailed outline of the nine (9) SLICE procedures is provided in Appendix D, where
         the critical operations with the flames are entirely contained within procedure 2.005.
         The flow calibration in procedure 2.003 was also important to the experimental
         results, where the calibration was regularly conducted, e.g., during each session of
         testing, in order to correlate the fan voltage with the velocity in the duct. The velocity
         was measured by an anemometer permanently mounted in the flow duct (upstream
         of the flame location) which had a time response that was too slow to be particularly
         useful during the flame testing. The calibration was carried out repeatedly because
         air flow through the duct was seen to diminish over time because of soot
         accumulation in the exhaust plenum. The other seven procedures are generally not
         important for interpreting the SLICE results because they deal with (1) the hardware
         set up, maintenance, and stowage plus (2) the handling of the camera media.

         It should be understood that the SLICE procedures apply to both the SLICE and
         SPICE experiments because additional SPICE tests were conducted in 2012,
         effectively as part of SLICE. In 2012, the SPICE tests accounted for approximately
         one quarter of the combined total. The only SPICE-specific procedure is part 4 of
         procedure 2.005, i.e., the Smoke Point Flame Test. Part 2 of the same procedure,
         the Standard Flame Test, is for SLICE. However, this flame portion of the testing
         was always conducted per verbal direction from the ground, so the SLICE and
         SPICE procedures were effectively identical. As previously described, Dr. David L.
         Urban, the SPICE PI, directed Pettit during the SPICE tests instead of Stocker.

         The final procedure, 4.001, was developed after the on-orbit set up of the hardware
         on Jan. 20, 2012 when it was discovered that the video data overlay was incorrectly
         scrolling hexadecimal characters. The video overlay was critical to the experiments
         because it was used to both display and record the numerical data, e.g., the fuel and
         air flow conditions. The scrolling display mode occurred because an integrated
         battery had completely discharged since the hardware was last used in 2009.
         Although the battery was recharged, a sequence of temporary electrical connections
         had to be made - in the absence of switches - to return the overlay to the proper
         display mode. The overlay was reset when the procedure was successfully carried
         out by Pettit on Feb. 6 GMT 037), after which testing began on Feb. 9 (GMT 040).
         The SPLICE testing continued twice per week until March 23 when the last of the
         fuel was consumed. For reference, the SPICE hardware was next used for the
         Burning and Suppression of Solids (BASS) experiment, where it began just days
         after SLICE’s completion.

      a. Crew Training
         Although SLICE (including the 2012 SPICE tests) was conducted completely by Don
         Pettit, several crew members were each provided with one hands-on training session
         on SLICE, SPICE, and BASS, i.e., the trio of experiments that were conducted
         together in the MSG using the same hardware and similar procedures. The training
         was conducted at NASA Johnson in Building 5 South (i.e., 5S) in a training version of
         the MSG located within a mock-up of a space station module; see Figure 9. Chuck
         Bunnell led the crew training, and Dennis Stocker represented SLICE in each of
         those sessions.

         Astronauts Ron Garan (Increments 27-28) and Mike Fossum (Increments 28-29)
         were trained in Oct. 2010, Don Pettit (Increments 30-31) was trained in March 2011,
         and Joe Acaba (Increments 31-32) and Sunita Williams (Increments 32-33) were
         trained in July 2011. Through this training, at least one U.S. crewmember was
         trained from increment 27-33, i.e., from April 2011 to Nov. 2012, where SLICE was
         conducted in the middle of that range. However, SLICE had been scheduled for
         earlier operation, where Stocker (for example) had expected that it would have been
         conducted by Mike Fossum, who was on orbit from June to Nov. 2011. But SLICE
         had to wait for its turn in the MSG, where SLICE was preceded by MSG experiments
         using the Selectable Optical Diagnostics Instrument (SODI) which is a modular mini-
         facility that was developed by the European Space Agency (ESA).

         Each crewmember was trained for only a few hours (total) roughly a year before their
         respective launch, but this wasn’t a significant issue for the three experiments, i.e.,
         SLICE, SPICE, and BASS. They had the distinct advantage that the science portion
         of the testing was always conducted with verbal direction from the ground, where the
         direction was from Stocker in the case of SLICE. Direct communication by
         researchers on space-to-ground is unusual, but it became an established approach
         for SPICE during its 2009 testing. That approach was taken when much of the fuel
         was wasted when SPICE operations first began and was subsequently adopted by
         both SLICE and BASS with full support from the crew.

      d. Video Overlay Anomaly
         During the on-orbit hardware set up on Jan. 20, 2012, it was discovered that the
         experiment’s video data overlay was scrolling hexadecimal characters rather than
         displaying the test conditions (e.g., fuel and air flows), etc. This critical data was not
         otherwise recorded or visible to either Pettit or the ground team directing the time-
         sensitive experiment operations. Therefore, conducting the experiment in this
         condition would have been much more difficult, and would have resulted in a very
         large loss of science for the SLICE and SPICE experiments, as well as the
         subsequent BASS experiment which used the same hardware. The fuel flow rate
         and air velocity would have had to have been based on the settings of the respective
         potentiometers, i.e., for the fan voltage in the latter case. And the fan calibration
         planned for every session (i.e., day of testing) would not have been possible.
         Researchers would have had to rely on the calibrations from the 2009 SLICE
         operations, in which the air velocity was seen to decay from session to session
         because of soot accumulation.            In short, operations would have become
         cumbersome and knowledge of the test conditions would have been approximate at
         best.

         Through testing with the ground hardware in the following days, it was determined
         that the problem was caused by a completely drained battery on the titler board,
         where the titler remained stuck in a troubleshooting mode after the battery was
         recharged. A corrective procedure was developed by Jay Owens, Dennis Stocker,
         and John E. Thomas (of NASA Glenn), who had been the electrical engineer for both
         SPICE and the preceding ELF investigation, with help from Bob Hawersaat and Tibor
         Lorik. The recovery procedure was formalized and the plan was approved, e.g.,
         based on the summary charts included in the appendices. The ISS On-Orbit Status
         Report for Feb. 4 at:
             http://www.nasa.gov/directorates/heo/reports/iss_reports/2012/02042012.html
         references this progress in a message from Stocker to Pettit:
             “Don, the SLICE team has developed a procedure to reset the video overlay from

            the hexadecimal trouble-shooting mode in which it is currently stuck to the
            normal display of data (e.g., flow conditions). We look forward to the conduct of
            that procedure and the start of testing next week.”

      e. On-Orbit Testing
         There were 13 sessions (i.e., days) of testing not counting the gathering (on Jan. 19)
         and set-up (on Jan. 20) of the hardware and the recovery of the video overlay (on
         Feb. 6), i.e., where there were 16 days of operations when those days were
         included. The testing which began on Feb. 9 (GMT 040) and ended on March 23
         (GMT 083) as summarized in Table 6, in which the fuel(s) used are identified, and
         other information is provided. However, the burner tubes used are not listed in the
         summary table because they were regularly changed throughout the testing.

         In the 13 sessions of testing, all 12 bottles of the gaseous fuel were completely
         consumed, where it was almost always one complete bottle emptied per session. To
         be clear, the sessions were almost always ended when the bottle was emptied, i.e.,
         as opposed to continuing to another fuel bottle. There were only two sessions
         where the bottle wasn’t emptied, on Feb. 24 (GMT 055) and March 5 (GMT 065).
         And there was only one session, on March 19 (GMT 079), during which more than
         one fuel and fuel bottle was used.

         The fan calibration was carried out on every day of testing plus on Feb. 6 (GMT 037),
         when the video overlay was reset. It was normally just conducted once at the end of
         the testing, but it was conducted twice during some sessions, e.g., Feb. 13 (GMT
         044) and March 19 (GMT 077). This will be discussed further in the next section on
         the coflow uncertainty. Meanwhile, the absolute light calibration, i.e., the pyrometry
         operation, was conducted four times, twice on Feb. 13 (GMT 044), once on Feb. 17
         (GMT 048), and once on March 19 (GMT 079). This calibration was carried out to
         allow determination of the soot volume fraction from the photography with the
         blackbody-characterized digital SLR camera.

         Dark frame reference photographs were taken each day with the digital SLR camera,
         where two sets were taken on March 1 (GMT 061). In addition, operational
         photographs of Pettit conducting SPLICE tests were taken on Feb. 24, March 5, and
         March 9 (i.e., GMT 055, 065, 069). Meanwhile, fisheye photographs of the SPICE
         hardware set up were taken through the MSG’s left gloveport on Feb. 20 and 27
         (GMT 051 and 058), where that gloveport was normally sealed with a blank.

         The 13 sessions included 8 sessions for SLICE (with verbal direction by Stocker), 1
         session for SPICE (with verbal direction by Urban), and 4 sessions with tests for both
         experiments (with verbal direction by both Stocker and Urban). Collectively, there
         were roughly 120 ignitions. Over 4,000 high-resolution digital photographs were
         taken, which included roughly 700 bracketed sets of flame photographs. These
         bracketed photo sets were almost always at different test (e.g., flow) conditions,
         where that is arguably the best indication of the quantity of data collected.

         days to allow time for the still image data from the preceding session to be
         downlinked and given a cursory review so that its results could be used to guide
         subsequent testing. With this approach, testing in a given week occurred on Monday
         or Tuesday and then again on Thursday or Friday. However, when testing was
         conducted on Friday and the subsequent Monday, the image data from Friday
         unfortunately wasn’t available on the Digital Imagery Management System (DIMS)
         for review prior to the Monday testing. [For reference, DIMS has now been

      f. Coflow Uncertainty
         There are unfortunately significant uncertainties in the velocity of the coflowing air in
         the SLICE experiment. For example, the fan calibration conducted at the end of the
         first session of testing (on Feb. 9, i.e., GMT 040) indicated much greater flows than
         those of any subsequent days or the end of the preceding session (on Feb. 6, GMT
         037), which included both the video overlay reset and the soot removal procedure,
         as seen in Figure 22.

         While the anomalous Feb. 9 (GMT 040) results appear to be an outlier, they were
         similar to those measured after the SPICE soot removal conducted in 2009,
         suggesting the possibility of a common cause. Furthermore, fluctuations can be
         seen in the rest of the plot, suggesting uncertainty regardless of the GMT 040
         results, although the general trend was decreasing air velocity with time which is
         reasonable given the known degradation of the flow with the accumulation of soot.

         Given the importance of the coflow velocity on the flame behavior, especially under
         lifted flame conditions, its measurement and uncertainty are discussed here.

         Pre-launch Characterization
         The SPICE hardware used to conduct the SLICE experiment is still on orbit where it
         has been used for the BASS and BASS-II experiments. As a result, the most recent
         characterization of the spaceflight hardware was conducted in June 2008 prior to its
         launch.

         Coflow Asymmetry
         Lifted flames are very sensitive to flow non-uniformities and the presence of an
         asymmetry in coflow of the on-orbit SPICE hardware during SLICE operations is
         strongly suggested by the following:
         (a) the observation during SLICE set up on Jan. 20, 2012 that the duct screen within
         the flow duct and upstream of the burner tube outlet had become dislodged (i.e.,
         pushed out of place) and was not orthogonal to the burner/duct axis,
         (b) the lack of axisymmetric lifted flames in SLICE testing in 2012 despite the
         presence of axisymmetric lifted SLICE flames, e.g., in Figure 6, in exploratory testing
         in 2009 (during SPICE operations),
         (c) inconsistent on-orbit flow characterization results, and
         (d) an anomaly with the Burning and Suppression of Solids II (BASS-II) experiment
         during its on-orbit operations.

         Dislodged Duct Screen and Asymmetric Flames (a and b)
         Although astronaut Don Pettit pushed the duct screen back into what nominally
         appeared to be the correct position (i.e., addressing the first asymmetry issue), the
         base of the lifted 2012 flames were significantly tilted relative to the burner/duct axis,
         whereas the flame base was barely tilted in the exploratory 2009 testing. The tilted
         base of the lifted SLICE flames strongly suggests that the fan-driven air flow was
         somewhat asymmetric during the 2012 SLICE and SPICE tests. It is certainly
         possible that the dislodging of the duct screen created an asymmetric opening in the
         screen around the burner tube holder that remained when the screen was pushed
         back into place. For reference, note that the hardware was stowed on ISS during the
         years between the completion of 2009 SPICE testing and the 2012 set up of SLICE.

         Inconsistent On-Orbit Flow Characterization (c)
         Based on the 2009 SPICE experience, the SLICE operations included measurement
         of a reference air velocity as a function of the fan voltage. The purpose was to
         determine the possible reduction in flow, if any, caused by soot accumulation in the
         duct’s exhaust plenum (i.e., soot trap) which could then be removed and cleaned if
         needed. The measurement was made at several fan settings using a TSI 8475-3 air
         velocity probe which was integrated at a fixed point in the flow duct upstream of both
         the duct screen (H012) and the burner tube (H032) in Figure 18. As previously
         discussed, this characterization was carried out on every day of SLICE testing and
         sometimes twice.

         Other than the regular characterization, the velocity measured by the probe was
         generally ignored during the SLICE operations because its response time was too
         slow for the changes made in the fan setting. As a result, the primary use for the
         probe was to track the decay in the coflow with soot accumulation.

         The air velocity did drop with soot accumulation as expected, e.g., on days with
         SPICE tests. It dropped precipitously on the final day of SLICE testing, GMT 083
         (i.e., March 23), during which tests were conducted beyond the smoke point, i.e.,

                                              40
SLICE Final Report (2017)                                                     2017-DEC-26

         where unburnt soot streamed out of the flame’s tip. However, some irregularities
         were observed in the measurements, where the air velocity measured at the same
         fan setting was seen to increase from one day to the next rather than (a) decrease
         because of soot accumulation or (b) remain constant. These were generally minor,
         e.g., with nominal increases or 1 cm/s or less, with the exception of GMT 040 (i.e.,
         Feb. 9, 2012), when the velocity was as much as 10 cm/s greater than in the
         preceding and subsequent characterizations. This can be seen in the subsequent
         plot, where the cause for the increased air velocity on GMT 040 has not been
         identified. On-orbit testing to elucidate the cause indicated that the much higher
         GMT 040 flow wasn’t the result of the glovebox ports being open, the glovebox
         circulation being active, or the door to the SPICE flow duct being open (where it
         opens to allow burner tube and igniter exchange).

         BASS-II Anomaly (d)
         While the dislodged screen might have caused flow asymmetry within the duct even
         after it was nominally pushed back into place, another possibility became evident in
         the BASS-II testing conducted on ISS in 2014. The on-orbit anomaly in which the
         SPICE hardware overheated and a leak became apparent.                  The BASS-II
         observations strongly suggest the presence of air leakage at the flow duct’s door,
         which opens with a hinge to allow replacement of the igniter or burner tube (in the
         case of SLICE and SPICE). There is no gasket sealing the gap between door and
         the duct, making the seal reliant on the door snugly fitting against the flow duct.
         Therefore, the door might not seal well if the hinge or latch mechanisms became
         loose. While the leak almost certainly grew during the anomaly, e.g., because of the
         high temperatures causing the door to bow, it may have been present at a smaller
         size during the 2012 SLICE testing. Inconsistent sealing of the door might explain
         the variations in the air velocity in Figure 22. It is furthermore possible that door
         leakage led to the asymmetric lifted flames, where the door could have loosed since
         the 2009 SPICE operations.

         Characterization of the Ground Hardware
         Additional characterization was carried out for SLICE by Bin Ma with the ground
         hardware that was used for engineering, crew training, and normal-gravity testing.
         This ground hardware was originally built for the Enclosed Laminar Flames (ELF)
         investigation, which was conducted on the STS-87 space shuttle mission in 1997,
         and was subsequently upgraded for SPICE. The air flow was characterized in
         December 2009 at a variety of positions within the duct, both with and without the
         burner tube installed. Detailed measurements were later made just 1 mm
         downstream of the 1.6-mm burner tube outlet. That later characterization is
         discussed in the following paper including the supplemental material:
             B. Ma, S. Cao, D. Giassi, D.P. Stocker, F. Takahashi, B.A.V. Bennett, M.D.
             Smooke, and M.B. Long, “An experimental and computational study on soot
             formation in a coflow jet flame under microgravity and normal gravity,” presented
             at the 35th International Symposium on Combustion, San Francisco, CA, 3-8
             August, 2014.
         However, it is important to recognize that there may be differences in the flow
         characteristics of the ground and spaceflight versions of the hardware, where even
         minor differences could make lifted flames asymmetric, perhaps especially in
         microgravity.

      g. Hardware Return
         The hardware which wasn’t needed for the continuing BASS operations returned to
         Earth in the unmanned Dragon cargo vehicle on SpaceX-1 mission on Oct. 28, 2012.
         Although it was the third Dragon mission, it was the first official ISS cargo mission by
         a private corporation, where the first two were considered demonstration flights.
         However, the second demonstration mission, in May 2012, had already successfully
         transported cargo to and from the space station. Following its return to Cleveland,
         the hardware, including the empty fuel bottles and the pyrometry fiber arrays, was
         put into bonded storage at NASA Glenn.

         Many burnt BASS samples were returned on the same Dragon flight. Another
         returned component was the digital SLR camera used in the 2009 SPICE tests, i.e.,
         the Nikon D100, given that it was replaced by SLICE with a blackbody-characterized
         D300s for determination of the soot temperature and volume fraction.

      h. Normal-Gravity Testing and Modeling
         Normal-gravity (1g) tests were conducted in the ground hardware following the
         completion of the on-orbit operations so that the effect of buoyancy could be
         assessed through comparison of the 1g and microgravity results. This was primarily
         carried out by Bin Ma at Yale University as part of his doctoral research, where some
         testing was also conducted at NASA Glenn.

         Computational modeling, which is still ongoing, has been carried out at Yale by
         graduate student Su (Andy) Cao under the leadership of Prof. Mitch Smooke, where
         Dr. Beth Anne Bennett has also contributed to this important effort.

         B. Ma, S. Cao, D. Giassi, D.P. Stocker, F. Takahashi, B.A.V. Bennett, M.D. Smooke,
         and M.B. Long, “An experimental and computational study on soot formation in a
         coflow jet flame under microgravity and normal gravity,” Proceedings of the
         Combustion Institute, Vol. 35, Issue 1, pp. 839-846 (2015).

            Abstract: “Upon the completion of the Structure and Liftoff in Combustion
            Experiment (SLICE) in March 2012, a comprehensive and unique set of
            microgravity coflow diffusion flame data was obtained. This data covers a range
            of conditions from weak flames near extinction to strong, highly sooting flames,
            and enabled the study of gravitational effects on phenomena such as liftoff,
            blowout and soot formation. The microgravity experiment was carried out in the
            Microgravity Science Glovebox (MSG) on board the International Space Station
            (ISS), while the normal gravity experiment was performed at Yale utilizing a copy
            of the flight hardware. Computational simulations of microgravity and normal
            gravity flames were also carried out to facilitate understanding of the
            experimental observations. This paper focuses on the different sooting behaviors
            of CH4 coflow jet flames in microgravity and normal gravity. The unique set of
            data serves as an excellent test case for developing more accurate
            computational models. Experimentally, the flame shape and size, lift-off height,
            and soot temperature were determined from line-of-sight flame emission images
            taken with a color digital camera. Soot volume fraction was determined by
            performing an absolute light calibration using the incandescence from a flame-
            heated thermocouple. Computationally, the MC-Smooth vorticity–velocity
            formulation was employed to describe the chemically reacting flow, and the soot
            evolution was modeled by the sectional aerosol equations. The governing
            equations and boundary conditions were discretized on an axisymmetric
            computational domain by finite differences, and the resulting system of fully
            coupled, highly nonlinear equations was solved by a damped, modified Newton’s
            method. The microgravity sooting flames were found to have lower soot
            temperatures and higher volume fraction than their normal gravity counterparts.
            The soot distribution tends to shift from the centerline of the flame to the wings
            from normal gravity to microgravity.”

      b. Benefits
         The twin purposes for the SLICE research were increased fuel efficiency and
         reduced pollutant emission in practical combustion devices on Earth. More explicitly,
         the experiment was conducted to advance combustion modeling capability, which
         “allows system designers to improve efficiency and reduce harmful pollutants in ways
         never before possible” (as stated by an external reviewer of the SLICE experiment).
         In other words, the SLICE research was conducted to advance energy technology
         and improve our global environment. It is also plausible that SLICE results could
         benefit one or more space exploration technologies, e.g., waste processing or
         spacecraft fire safety, but that was not the intent of the research.

         SLICE has provided a unique set of data to validate coflow diffusion flame numerical
         models, such as that of SLICE Co-Investigator Mitchell Smooke et al., described for
         example in SLICE papers [1-2]. From [1], “… the MC-Smooth vorticity-velocity
         formulation was employed to describe the reactive gaseous mixture, and soot
         evolution was modeled by sectional aerosol equations. The governing equations and
         boundary conditions were discretized on a two-dimensional computational domain by
         finite differences, and the resulting set of fully coupled, strongly nonlinear equations
         was solved simultaneously at all points using a damped, modified Newton's method.”

         By comparing numerical predictions with corresponding experimental
         measurements, the numerical model and submodels (e.g., thermal radiation, soot
         formation, chemical mechanisms) can be evaluated and validated over a much
         broader range of flame conditions than would be available under normal-gravity
         conditions. Validated by SLICE measurements, the numerical model provides


                                             45
SLICE Final Report (2017)                                                            2017-DEC-26

         information that was not measured experimentally, e.g., the velocity field, heat
         release rate, mixture fraction, and consumption rate of the individual species. This
         information can help improve our understanding of the complicated effects of
         buoyancy on flame structure and the interactions between the flow field and chemical
         reactions. The novel SLICE data also serves as a benchmark to optimize the
         computations. While simulating CH4 flames, the SLICE researchers have improved
         the predictive capability of the numerical model through optimization of its boundary
         conditions and evaluation of the performance of several kinetic reaction
         mechanisms. Furthermore, in conjunction with the SLICE data, the numerical model
         is being tested over a large set of parameter ranges, where this effort is expected to
         help generate more reliable predictions for ACME’s CLD Flame experiment. This
         improved capability can help researchers optimize the CLD Flame test matrix and
         thus maximize its scientific return.

         While soot pyrometry was successfully accomplished in the SLICE experiment, the
         experiment led to refined pyrometry requirements for the five ACME experiments.
         More specifically, SLICE led to a new specification for the BG-7 filter transmission,
         where that filter will be important for equalizing the intensity in all three channels (i.e.,
         red, blue, and green) of the Gigabit Ethernet (GigE) camera, which will prevent data
         loss (from saturation) and improve the data quality (e.g., signal to noise ratio). This
         is important for both soot pyrometry and Thin Filament Pyrometry (TFP), where
         those are ACME’s primary techniques to measure the temperature field.

         In addition, the results of the SLICE experiment will help enable optimization of the
         camera settings for the ACME experiments. SLICE provided preliminary results on
         the soot volume fraction, which (along with the flame intensity) is often much greater
         in microgravity than normal gravity. Those results combined with a quantitative
         comparison of the sensitivities of the SLICE and ACME cameras (accounting for the
         associated optics and optical layouts) will guide the selection of appropriate ACME
         camera settings.

         The CLD Flame test matrix is also being reviewed in light of the SLICE results,
         where that process could lead to refinement of the test matrix so as to optimize CLD
         Flame’s scientific return.

                   Structure & Liftoff In Combustion Experiment (SLICE)
           Principal Investigator: Marshall B. Long, Yale University, New Haven, CT
      Project Scientist: Dennis P. Stocker, NASA Glenn Research Center, Cleveland, OH

                         Smoke Point In Coflow Experiment (SPICE)
     Principal Investigator: David L. Urban, NASA Glenn Research Center, Cleveland, OH

      SLICE and SPICE use the same experimental hardware and nearly identical
      procedures but have different science objectives. Together they came to be
      informally referred to as SPLICE which could serve as an acronym for Smoke
      Point & Liftoff In Coflow Experiment. Roughly a quarter of the subject testing
      was for SPICE.


I.   Introduction
     The SLICE experiment investigates the structure of lifting and lifted flames, where flow
     conditions and the combustion chemistry cause the flame to detach from the burner and
     stabilize at a downstream position. It is a precursor to the Coflow Laminar Diffusion Flame
     (CLD Flame) experiment, where the SLICE results will be used to maximize the scientific
     return of that upcoming space station experiment. The SPICE experiment is an outgrowth
     of the Laminar Soot Processes (LSP) experiment which flew on STS-83, STS-94, and
     STS-107. SPICE investigates the smoke point phenomena in low gravity, a topic that was
     the subject of controversy before LSP established the clear existence of smoke points in
     low gravity. SPICE extends these results to more conditions and fuel compounds.

     A.   Hypothesis
          SLICE has a computational flame model, developed by Co-Investigator Mitchell D.
          Smooke et al. (of Yale University), which effectively served as the experiment’s
          hypotheses. And based on previous research, it was known that higher forced

                                              50
SLICE Final Report (2017)                                                        2017-DEC-26

         velocities can be required to lift microgravity flames because of the absence of
         buoyant convection. The hypothesis of the SPICE investigation was that smoke
         point behavior will be observed for co-flowing flames and that it will show
         dependence on nozzle diameter and coflow velocity.

    B.   Objectives of Investigation
         The goal of the SLICE experiment was to improve our understanding of the physical
         and chemical processes controlling diffusion (i.e., non-premixed) flame structure and
         lifting phenomena (i.e., stabilization) and to provide for rigorous testing of numerical
         models including thermal radiation, soot formation, and detailed chemical kinetics.
         Good agreement between experimental and computational results has been
         demonstrated for lifted flames at moderate flame conditions, but that agreement
         breaks down when the fuel is highly diluted or the soot production is high. SLICE is a
         precursor for the Coflow Laminar Diffusion Flame (CLD Flame) experiment, which is
         a part of the Advanced Combustion via Microgravity Experiments (ACME) project
         that is currently in development for conduct in the Combustion Integrated Rack
         (CIR). A common goal of the SLICE and CLD Flame experiments is to improve
         computational techniques such that a broader range of flame conditions can be
         effectively modeled than is currently possible. SLICE’s specific objectives were as
         follows:
                  For diffusion flames of methane, ethylene, and selected nitrogen
                  dilutions of each fuel burning in a coflow of air:
                  (1) Characterize the structure of the flame, especially its base (i.e.,
                  stabilizing region), from attached through lifted conditions as a
                  function of the fuel, burner diameter, and flow conditions.
                  (2) Identify the liftoff velocity limits as a function of the fuel and burner
                  diameter.

         The objective of the SPICE experiment was to find the laminar smoke-point
         properties of nonbuoyant jet diffusion flames (i.e., the properties of the largest
         laminar jet diffusion flames that do not emit soot) for several fuels under different
         nozzle diameter / co-flow velocity combinations.           Luminous flame shape
         measurements were also made to verify models of the flame shapes under coflow
         conditions.

    C.   Previous Mission Experience
         The SPICE experiment was previously conducted in the Microgravity Science
         Glovebox (MSG) in early 2009 as part of Increments 18-20, where those operations
         included proof-of-concept testing for SLICE.

    D.   Background/History of Project (Optional)
         While there are significant differences, SPICE and especially SLICE have similarities
         to the Enclosed Laminar Flames (ELF) investigation which was conducted in the
         Middeck Glovebox (MGBX) on STS-87 in 1997. In fact, the experiment assembly
         used by the subject experiments was designed for the MGBX (and not the MSG) and
         is directly based on ELF’s design. Furthermore, the ground unit used for the SPLICE
         crew training, procedure and test matrix development, and other normal-gravity
         testing was originally used for those same purposes for ELF, although important
         upgrades were made.

         In terms of science, SPICE is directly related to the Laminar Soot Processes (LSP)

                                             51
SLICE Final Report (2017)                                                          2017-DEC-26

           experiment which was conducted on STS-83 (1997), STS-94 (1997) and STS-107
           (2003) in facility that was a precursor to the CIR. LSP studied laminar gas-jet
           diffusion flames in a quiescent atmosphere while SPICE is for similar flames in a
           coflow. Meanwhile, SLICE and ELF were both studies of lifting and lifted coflow
           laminar diffusion flames, where ELF was focused on determination of the liftoff and
           blowout limits whereas SLICE was focused on the structure of lifting and lifted
           flames. Furthermore, SLICE is a precursor to the Coflow Laminar Diffusion Flame
           (CLD Flame) experiment that is in development for conduct in the CIR as part of the
           Advanced Combustion via Microgravity Experiments (ACME) project.

II.   Methods/Research Operations

      A.   Discussion of Method/Protocol
           SLICE and SPICE are both crew-operated MSG experiments, where the
           crewmember was verbally directed by Stocker and Urban, respectively, during the
           testing based on the downlink of the experiment video. Experiment variables
           included the burner tube diameter, the gaseous fuel (including any dilution), the fuel
           flow, and the air coflow. The burner and fuel were fixed during each test, where the
           crewmember’s role after igniting the flame was to vary the flow conditions and take
           high-resolution still photos as directed. Burn times were typically on the order of 1
           minute in duration, and after every two burns the MSG work volume was flushed with
           ISS cabin air as a safety measure. At the end of each session, a calibration of the
           air coflow was conducted because it diminished over time as a result of soot
           accumulation in the exhaust plenum.

           An example of the experiment operations can be seen in the composite video of a
           single SLICE test at:
           http://www.nasa.gov/mission_pages/station/research/news/burming_questions.html
           The synchronized clip combines experiment video (of the flame), video of astronaut
           Don Pettit conducting the experiment, and the space/ground audio communication
           between Pettit and Dennis Stocker, the SLICE Project Scientist. As can be seen in
           this example, the flow was adjusted throughout the experiment so that
           measurements could be made of the flame structure at a variety of flow conditions
           with the selected burner and fuel combination. Typically, the fuel was held fixed
           (after a post-ignition adjustment), but in this example, the coflow was held fixed while
           the fuel flow was increased. Generally, the flow was slowly increased as the flame
           approached the liftoff limit where it detaches from the burner and in some cases
           stabilizes above the burner but often blows out (i.e., extinguishes) as in this example.
           The liftoff and blowout limits are determined from analysis of the experiment video
           while the still photographs are used to determine the flame structure, including the
           soot temperature and volume fraction. The computation of the latter also relied on a
           pyrometry calibration procedure, conducted during three sessions, in which Pettit
           photographed silicon carbide fibers heated by a flame.

           Most SPICE tests were conducted by setting the coflow velocity, igniting the flame,
           and adjusting the fuel flow rate. Approximate smoke points were identified in the
           video by the flight crew and simultaneously by the ground support crew in Cleveland.
           After the SPICE tests, the video record was analyzed to identify smoke point
           conditions. The associated coflow and fuel flow rates were obtained from digital data
           recorded in the video. The smoke point lengths were obtained from the video record
           and are the measured distance from the burner tip to the tip of the luminous flame on

SLICE Final Report (2017)                                                          2017-DEC-26

            the flame axis.

III.   Results

       A.   List of Preflight, In-flight, Postflight Anomalies
            The most significant anomaly was discovered when the hardware was set up in the
            MSG (on GMT 2012/020). It was found that the experiment’s video data overlay had
            become stuck in a troubleshooting mode where it was scrolling hexadecimal
            characters rather than displaying the test conditions (e.g., fuel and air flows), etc.
            This critical data was not otherwise recorded or visible to the ground team directing
            the time-sensitive experiment operations. Therefore, conducting the experiment in
            this condition would have been much more difficult, and would have resulted in a
            very large loss of science for the SLICE and SPICE experiments, as well as the
            subsequent Burning And Suppression of Solids (BASS) experiment which uses the
            same hardware. Fortunately, a corrective procedure was developed by the SLICE
            team which was successfully implemented by Don Pettit (on GMT 2012/037). Pettit
            reset the overlay to its normal mode by connecting test leads to the experiment’s
            video titler board and making a sequence of momentary electrical connections,
            mimicking the operation of a switch panel that was not part of the spaceflight
            hardware. The anomaly occurred because a battery on the titler board had
            discharged since the hardware’s last use in 2009, where recharging of the battery
            alone did not return the overlay to the desired mode.

            A comparatively minor in-flight anomaly was the outcome of the fan calibration
            conducted at the end of the first session of testing (on GMT 2012/040). Those
            results indicated much greater flows than those of any subsequent days or the end of
            the preceding session (on GMT 2012/037) which included both the video overlay
            reset and the soot removal procedure. While the anomalous results appear to be an
            outlier, they were similar to those measured after the SPICE soot removal conducted
            in 2009, suggesting the possibility of a common cause. Nonetheless, the reason for
            the anomalous higher flow readings is still not understood, although some simple
            tests were conducted as part of the in-flight operations to assess possible causes.

       B.   Completeness/Quality of Data
            There were 13 sessions (i.e., days) of testing not counting the gathering and set-up
            of the hardware and the recovery of the video overlay, i.e., such that there a total of
            16 sessions. In the 13 sessions, all 12 bottles of the gaseous fuel were completely
            consumed, where it was almost always one complete bottle emptied per session. As
            such, the data collection was complete for SLICE and SPICE.

            The 13 sessions included 8 sessions for SLICE, 1 session for SPICE, and 4 sessions
            with tests for both experiments. Collectively, over 100 tests were conducted (i.e.,
            flames were ignited) and over 4,000 high-resolution digital photographs were taken.
            Those 14-megapixel photos included roughly 700 bracketed sets of flames photos,
            which were almost always at different test (e.g., flow) condition, where that is
            arguably the best indication of the quantity of data collected.

            The data quality was high with the exception that the flame images revealed a flow
            asymmetry, presumably in the coflow (and not the fuel flow) that caused the lifted
            flames to be always tilted, where that wasn’t observed in the SLICE proof-of-concept
            testing conducted as part of the SPICE operations in 2009. Some tilt is acceptable if

                                                53
SLICE Final Report (2017)                                                        2017-DEC-26

         the flame is near (e.g., within 1 cm of) the burner, but this was disappointing. During
         the initial experiment set up, it was observed that a screen upstream of the burner tip
         had been pushed out of position. While Pettit corrected this to the best of his ability,
         it is possible that dislocation of the screen may have been the cause of the flow and
         thus flame asymmetry. Alternately, it might have been caused by air leakage around
         the experiment assembly’s front door which may have loosened since 2009.

    C.   Status of Data Analysis
         The SLICE and SPICE testing resulted in a comprehensive data set with a variety of
         test conditions, including seven hydrocarbon fuels (accounting for various nitrogen
         dilutions), five fuel nozzle sizes, and a spectrum of flow conditions. A fully-
         characterized color digital camera was used to image line-of-sight flame emissions to
         obtain flame properties such as the flame shape and size, lift-off height, as well as
         soot temperature and volume fraction. The data analysis carried out thus far includes
         quantitative determination of soot temperature and volume fraction for all sooty
         flames and lift-off height measurements for lifted flames. The data analysis is still
         ongoing, where a major remaining task is the conduct of comparison normal-gravity
         (1g) tests. While many 1g tests were conducted in advance of the ISS testing, the
         latter were conducted over a broader range of flow conditions. Numerical simulation
         of the flames will also be performed and compared with experimental results.

         The SPICE data have been collected and transferred to the University of Maryland
         for detailed analysis by a graduate student employee. This analysis will consist of
         careful review of the video record to identify images at the smoke point. These
         images will then be used to determine the instantaneous flame size and the
         associated test conditions for that time will be extracted from the data.


    D.   Preliminary Research Findings
         Due to the lack of buoyancy force, the microgravity (0g) flames are generally wider
         and less likely to lift off than their 1 g counterparts, and flames with higher dilution
         levels can be stabilized in microgravity. The soot residence time is also greatly
         enhanced in microgravity, which leads to lower soot temperatures and greater soot
         volume fractions.
